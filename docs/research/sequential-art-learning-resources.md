# Sequential Art Learning Resources: Books, Courses, Videos and Tutorials on the Craft of Graphic Novels

*Research date: 2026-11-17. Scope: educational resources that teach the craft of comics and graphic novels, sorted into three categories: **page layout and pacing** (§1), **text and dialogue integration** (§2) and **visual storytelling elements** (§3). Each entry gives the title, the author or creator, the format, a skill level, the component it teaches best and the page it was verified against. Each category ends with a suggested study sequence. §4 lists the leads that were dropped and why. §5 lists what is inferred and not confirmed.*

**How the sources were verified**

- Every entry was checked against a first-party page: the publisher's catalogue page, the author's or creator's own site, the course platform's own course page, or the official YouTube channel. Listicles, retailer pages, review blogs and course aggregators were used only to find leads and are not cited.
- The Source column links the page that was actually opened. The summary of an entry rests on what that page says (publisher description, syllabus, lesson list or the tutorial text), not on the reputation of the resource. Where the page gave only a blurb and no table of contents, the summary says no more than the blurb does.
- Pages were read through a fetch tool that returns an extraction of the page, and in some cases through a browser pane. Counts, prices and lesson lists are as returned on 2026-11-17.
- **Level** is a curriculum judgment unless marked *(stated)*, which means the source page itself names the level or the audience.
- **Format** is one of book, course, video or written tutorial. A free resource is marked as free.
- Some first-party sites could not be opened (a bot-check page, a blocked domain, a dead link). Those resources were verified on another first-party page or dropped; §4 says which.

Cross-references: *Effective Storytelling and Design Tips* (§2) has a section on panel composition and pacing. *Storytelling with The Odyssey of Sergeant Jack Brennan* (§3) has a unit on panel size and shape and on composing the page. *Marvel's The Art of Storytelling* and *Creating a Comic Page* (§3) each have lessons on panel layout.

**Suggested sequence**

1. Start with *Understanding Comics* for closure and time, then do the Cartooning Workout or Brunetti's first lessons the same week, so that the theory is tested on index cards at once.
2. Read the Art Rocket article and the dw-wp quickguides before drawing a first full page: they cover gutters, panel types, live area and bleeds, the things that are costly to fix later.
3. Work through *Drawing Words and Writing Pictures* or one of the two courses as the spine, and use McCloud's *Making Comics* and Eisner next to it for the reasons behind each choice.
4. Then study finished pages: Strip Panel Naked, *99 Ways* and *Panel Discussions* show the same decisions made by professionals.
5. Leave Groensteen and Cohn until there is a body of pages to test the theory against.

Cross-references: *Drawing Words and Writing Pictures* (§1) has lettering among its chapter subjects. Lesson 7 of Schoolism's *Introduction to Comics* (§1) is "Comic Book Lettering". *Panel Discussions* (§1) discusses balloon placement and when a sound effect helps or distracts.

**Suggested sequence**

1. Read *Comic Book Grammar & Tradition* and the Comicraft glossary first. They give the conventions and the names, and every later resource assumes them.
2. Learn the mechanics in the tool at hand next: the Clip Studio article, the Procreate series, or the dw-wp hand-lettering guide.
3. Then study placement, because it decides reading order: Shooter's memo is short and strict, and the typography section of the Celsys page adds text size and balloon length.
4. Take one full treatment, Piekos's book or one of the two courses, and letter several real pages with it.
5. Finish with the Strip Panel Naked episodes and Eisner, which treat lettering and dialogue as part of the storytelling, not as a production step.

Emanata, camera angles, shot composition, colour theory, and light and shadow used to convey emotion. Rows are ordered as a learning path, beginner first.

| Title | Author/Creator | Format | Level | What it teaches best | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Cross-references: McCloud's *Understanding Comics* (§1) covers the psychology of line styles and colour, and *Making Comics* (§1) covers body language and facial expression. Strip Panel Naked (§1) has episodes on framing and scale, for example ["Framing Characters in Human Target"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SCICh_Hgmmg). *Panel Discussions* (§1) covers colour used for emotion and the spotting of blacks. *Mastering Comics* (§1) has colour among its subjects.

**Suggested sequence**

1. Begin with *Picture This* and the SAW course: both teach why a shape, an angle or a placement carries a feeling, with almost no drawing skill needed.
2. Add the vocabulary: the shot types of *The Marvel Way* and the symbols of the *Lexicon of Comicana*, with Cohn's list for the manga set.
3. Then composition in sequence: *Framed Ink* first, the Proko courses next, and Katz for the film grammar behind them.
4. Take colour after composition, because value does most of the work: Sotomayor's or Russell's course, then Wilson's free lesson.
5. Keep Gurney and Block for last. They are general references on light and on visual structure, and they repay reading once there are pages to apply them to.

## 5. What Is Inferred

- **Levels.** Every level without *(stated)* is a judgment made from the description of the resource.
- **Blurb-only summaries.** No table of contents was seen for *Understanding Comics*, *Making Comics* (McCloud's own pages list topics, not chapters), *Comics and Sequential Art*, *Graphic Storytelling and Visual Narrative*, *Cartooning*, *99 Ways to Tell a Story*, *Panel Discussions*, *Mastering Comics*, *The System of Comics*, *The Visual Language of Comics*, *The Essential Guide to Comic Book Lettering*, *Framed Ink*, *Film Directing: Shot by Shot*, *Color and Light*, *The Lexicon of Comicana* and *Picture This*. Their summaries follow the publisher's or the author's description. A table of contents was seen for *The Visual Story*, one chapter title for *How to Draw Comics the Marvel Way*, and a session or lesson list for every course except the LinkedIn Learning one, whose topics come from Comicraft's page. Proko's page for *Creating a Comic Page* shows only part of its 39 lessons.
- **McCloud's books** were verified on his own site, because the HarperCollins catalogue pages are blocked for both tools. His pages give the titles without subtitles.
- **The Visual Language of Comics** was verified on the author's lab site, because the Bloomsbury page is blocked for the fetch tool.
- **Color and Light** was verified on the author's blog; the publisher's product page was not found.
- **The dw-wp.com quickguides and videos** were verified only from the site's own post list, which gives a one-line description of each. The posts themselves, the *Mastering Comics* table of contents and the authors' page on *Drawing Words and Writing Pictures* answered with a bot-check page after the first requests.
- **Blambot's articles** carry no byline. The site's About page states that the site and its contents are Nate Piekos's, which is the basis for the attribution.
- **Strip Panel Naked.** The channel page does not name its host. The PanelxPanel shop page names "Strip Panel Naked host Hassan Otsmane-Elhaou". Six video titles were confirmed through YouTube's own embed-information service; "Line of Sight in Comics" and "Empty Space on a Comic Book Page" were seen only in the channel's video list.
- **"Lettering Comic Books with Adobe Illustrator"**: the syllabus is from Comicraft's page. Its level is a judgment, and its availability on LinkedIn Learning is in doubt.
- **The Lexicon of Comicana**: the publisher's page does not use the word "emanata"; it describes the symbols the term covers.
